# Looking Really Closely at Your Coffee Mug

Welcome back. Take your regular ballpoint pen, slide your fingers far back from the tip so you are using the **Zero-Tension Grip** we learned in Chapter 1, and place a blank sheet of paper in front of you. Set a common ceramic coffee mug right there on the table beside your paper. 

Do not pick up the pen yet. Just look at the mug. 

Right now, your brain is playing a trick on you. If you close your eyes and try to picture that mug, what do you see? You probably see a cylinder with a floating, perfectly circular loop attached to the side. You see a mental symbol. Your brain loves symbols because they are efficient. When you were five years old, you learned a shorthand way to represent a mug, and ever since then, your brain has slapped that mental stencil over every real cup you have ever encountered. You are no longer looking at the object; you are looking at your memory of the object. 

To draw what is actually there, you have to break through that mental stencil. You have to stop drawing what you *know* is there and start drawing what your eyes *actually see*.

## The Shape of Flatness

Here is the secret that shocks absolute beginners: from where you are sitting right now, the rim of that mug is not a circle. 

Tilt your head slightly and look at the opening at the top of your mug. Unless you are hovering directly above it like a ceiling fan, that opening is not round. It is squashed. It is an oval, a flattened ellipse. If you draw a round circle for the top of a mug that is sitting sideways or slightly below your eye level, your brain has won the argument against your eyes. You have drawn your memory instead of reality. 

Let us prove this to ourselves without picking up a pen just yet. Hold up your thumb at arm's length and close one eye. Measure the height of the mug's top opening using the tip of your thumb compared to its width. You will find that the opening is much wider than it is tall. It is compressed. 

```exercise room="80%"
Take your pen using your loose, zero-tension grip. Do not worry about making a masterpiece. Let us practice capturing the true, squashed shape of the top opening before we build the rest of the mug. Look only at the physical rim of the mug in front of you. Draw that top opening as a flat, compressed oval using overlapping, scribbly lines. Let your hand move around the loop two or three times to find the shape, embracing the wobble. Do not lift your pen.

answer: Check your drawing: if the top opening looks like a wide-open round dinner plate or a symmetrical circle, your brain tricked you into drawing a symbol. If it looks like a squashed, flattened oval that hugs the physical edge your eye is tracing, you successfully bypassed your memory.
```

When you look at the body of the mug, the same rule applies. Ignore the handle for a moment. Look only at the left edge and the right edge. Do they go straight down, or do they curve inward slightly toward the bottom? Are they symmetrical, or is one side subtly different? Your eyes will tell you the truth if you let them. 

## The Continuous Journey

In Chapter 1, we learned about the **Human Signature** — the idea that messy, overlapping lines give a drawing life, and that there are no mistakes, only new territory. Now we are going to combine that philosophy with a specific physical rule: **The Continuous Line**.

When you draw your mug today, you are not going to lift your pen from the paper from the moment you start until the moment you finish. 

Why? Because the moment you lift your pen, hesitation creeps in. You stop, you judge, you look at the gap, and your inner critic wakes up. By keeping the ink flowing in a continuous, meandering path — much like a bird building a nest out of messy twigs — you bypass the internal judge. You force your hand to keep pace with your eyes. 

```figure alt="A close-up of a hand holding a ballpoint pen resting on a paper, drawing a continuous, looping line that forms the side of a mug without ever lifting off the page." kind="show" src="02-looking-really-closely-at-your-coffee-mug-2.jpg"
```

Let us trace how this works in practice. Start at the top left corner of the rim. Move your pen in a flattened oval across the top. Without lifting, loop down the left side of the mug. When you hit the bottom, loop across the base. Go back up the right side. When you reach the top right of the rim, loop inward to trace the inner lip of the opening. Finally, loop out to the side to capture the handle, and wind your way back to where you started. 

If your hand wanders off course, do not stop. Do not scribble it out. Just loop around again, letting the lines build up until a dark, rich density forms along the true physical edge of the mug. 

```recap
Let us anchor this. When drawing a real object from observation, your brain wants to supply a symbol — a memory of what a mug looks like. You defeat this by looking exclusively at the physical edges and translating those compressed, asymmetrical shapes directly onto the paper. By keeping your pen on the page in a continuous, flowing journey, you prevent your inner critic from interrupting your vision.
```

## Drawing the Mug Live

Now it is time for your main exercise. Set your coffee mug on the table. Sit comfortably. Remember your zero-tension grip: fingers slid far back, hand loose like a wet noodle. 

You are going to spend the rest of your 15 minutes today completing one continuous-line drawing of this mug. 

```exercise room="100%"
Look only at your mug, not at your paper. Watch the outer boundary where the ceramic meets the air. Let your pen glide across the page, mirroring the movement of your eyes. Let your lines overlap and wander. Capture the squashed oval of the rim, the sloping sides, the flat base, and the awkward loop of the handle. Do not lift your pen from the paper once you begin. If you make a detour, turn it into extra texture. 

answer: Look at your finished piece. If it looks a little frantic, furry, or uneven, congratulations — you have captured the human signature. If you managed to keep your pen on the paper the entire time, you successfully silenced your inner critic and drew what your eyes actually saw rather than what your memory told you was there.
```
