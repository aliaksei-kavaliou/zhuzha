# Looking Really Closely at Your Coffee Mug

Grab a coffee mug. Place it right in front of you on the table. Take your ordinary ballpoint pen — remember, holding it loosely like a small bird that might fly away, with your wrist floating nicely above the page just like we practiced in chapter one — and get ready. 

Here is the secret problem of drawing. Your brain is a lazy, efficient machine. When you look at the coffee mug on your table, your brain does not actually look at it. Your brain says, "Ah, a coffee mug. I know what a mug looks like. It is a cylinder with a loop on the side. I have drawn thousands of these in my imagination since I was five years old. Let me take over." And instantly, your brain stops looking at the physical object right in front of your eyes. Your brain replaces the real, unique, slightly lopsided mug on your desk with a cartoon symbol of a mug stored away in your memory.

We are going to trick that lazy brain. We are going to force our eyes to actually see what is sitting right there.

```figure kind="show" alt="A close-up line drawing of a person's eyes looking intensely at a ceramic coffee mug, with dotted sightlines shooting straight from the eyes to the physical rim of the mug, ignoring the handle entirely for the moment." src="02-looking-really-closely-at-your-coffee-mug-1.jpg"
```

When you look at your coffee mug, what shape is the top opening? 

Your brain will tell you: "It is a circle." 

Lean your head down so your eyes are level with the top rim of the mug. Look at it. Is it a circle? Unless you are floating directly above it in a helicopter, it is not a circle at all. From your eye level, it is a flattened oval. It is a squashed, compressed loop. Your brain wants to draw a round circle because it *knows* mugs have round openings, but your eyes see a flat, wide ellipse. 

We are not going to draw what we *know* is there. We are going to draw what our eyes *actually see*. This is the golden rule of drawing from life. Turn off your brain's memory bank and turn on your eyeballs. 

Let us practice this looking game right now before we touch pen to paper. 

```exercise space="blank"
Look at your mug. Find the exact point where the handle meets the body of the mug at the top. Do not think "handle" — think "where that curved piece of clay connects to the cylinder." Now, trace the outer edge of the mug all the way down to the table with your eyes, very slowly, taking ten full seconds to travel just ten centimetres. 
```

Notice how much detail your eyes skip over when you rush. Your mug probably has tiny bumps, a slight lean to the left, a little glitch in the glaze where it settled in the kiln. Your brain edits all of those out. Your pen, however, loves those little imperfections. Those little wobbles are what make a drawing look real instead of like a stamp.

Now, we are going to do something terrifying. We are going to draw that mug, from life, in one single continuous line. 

You heard that right. You are going to put your pen down on the paper at any point on the mug's edge, and you are not going to lift the pen off the paper until the entire outline is finished. No lifting. No stopping. If your line wanders off into the wrong territory, do not panic and do not reach for an eraser — because we don't have one, and even if we did, we wouldn't use it. Remember from chapter one: mistakes are just lines, and we fix our course by *layering* new lines right over the old ones. A messy, overlapping, multi-layered line is full of energy. A single, sterile, timid line is dead.

```figure alt="A hilariously wobbly, chaotic line drawing of a coffee mug, covered in overlapping pen loops where the hand kept moving without stopping, looking full of character and life." src="02-looking-really-closely-at-your-coffee-mug-2.jpg"
```

Let us break down how to execute this continuous-line mug drawing without freezing up.

1. Pick a starting spot on the mug's physical edge. Let's say, the top left corner of the rim.
2. Put your pen on the paper. 
3. Move your eyes along the physical edge of the mug *at the exact same speed* as your hand moves the pen across the paper. Fast eyes mean fast pen; slow eyes mean slow pen. Let your eyes crawl along the contour, and let your hand follow like a faithful dog on a leash.
4. When you get to the handle, trace down the outside of the handle, around the bottom loop of the handle, back up the inside of the handle, and continue around the body of the mug. 
5. Keep going until you meet back up with where you started. Keep the pen touching the paper the whole time.

```exercise space="blank"
Place your pen on your paper. Without lifting it once, draw the outline of your coffee mug by forcing your eyes to trace the real object in front of you. Do not worry if the handle ends up floating in the middle of the mug or if the base is twice as wide as the top. Just keep the pen moving.
```

```recap
When you draw a real object, your brain tries to substitute a cartoon memory of that object. To beat this, you must force your eyes to trace the actual physical edges — noticing that circles look like flat ovals from the side, and that handles and curves have specific, weird angles. By drawing the whole thing in one continuous pen stroke without lifting your hand, you stop yourself from judging your work and let the natural, imperfect wobble of your hand capture the true life of the object.
```

Let's do it one more time, because the second time is always where the magic starts to happen. Your hand relaxes just a fraction more. Your brain gives up control a little earlier. 

Look at the mug. Ignore the word "mug." Look only at the lines, the curves, the way the light hits the side and the shadow falls away. Put your pen down. Do not lift it. Draw what you see.

```

Look at what you just put on that paper. It might look lopsided. It might look like it's melting slightly to the side. That is not a failure — that is your own human signature. That is a drawing made by a living person looking at the real physical world, not a machine churning out pixels. You looked at a real thing in your room, and you put it down on paper with an ordinary pen.
