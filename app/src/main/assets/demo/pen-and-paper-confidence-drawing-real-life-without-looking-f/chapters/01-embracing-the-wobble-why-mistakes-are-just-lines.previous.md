# Embracing the Wobble: Why Mistakes Are Just Lines

Welcome. If you are sitting there with a standard ballpoint pen in your hand, feeling slightly foolish because you believe you cannot draw, you are in the exact right place. Let us get one truth out of the way immediately: you are not here to create a masterpiece that belongs in a gallery. You are here to learn how to make marks on paper using an ordinary pen, and more importantly, you are here to learn how to not care when those marks turn out messy, uneven, or completely lopsided. 

Most people never learn to draw because they are terrified of making a mistake. They pick up a pen or a pencil, press down hard, and the moment a line wanders off where it was not supposed to go, their shoulders tighten up. They stop. They look for an eraser. If there is no eraser, they crumple the paper up and throw it away. We are going to unlearn that reaction completely over the next fifteen minutes.

Let us start by looking at how you are holding that pen. If your knuckles are turning white and your forearm feels as stiff as a piece of iron timber, you are holding it far too tightly. When we write grocery lists or sign our names on official documents, we grip the pen very close to the tip, pressing down with immense force because every letter must be neat and precise. 

Drawing with a pen is entirely different. Drop your shoulders away from your ears. Slide your fingers back along the barrel of the pen so you are holding it much further from the paper. 

Let us repeat that, because it feels deeply uncomfortable at first: slide your fingers back along the pen. Hold the barrel loosely, as if you are holding a small bird that might fly away if you squeeze too hard. Your hand should be relaxed, your wrist should be loose, and your entire arm should feel soft.

When you draw with a loose hand and a ballpoint pen, the ink does not always flow in a smooth, perfect highway. Sometimes it skips. Sometimes your hand wobbles because human hands naturally shake just a tiny bit. That wobble is not an error. That wobble is not a failure. That wobble is simply a line. 

Think of it this way: a drawing is nothing more than a collection of ink marks on a flat sheet of white paper. If a line goes left instead of right, the paper does not break, the world does not stop spinning, and you are not in trouble. It is just a line. If you make a mark that looks clumsy, the correct response is never to groan or stop. The correct response is to draw right over the top of it with another line. 

```exercise
Take a blank piece of paper and an ordinary pen. Hold the pen loosely, far back from the tip. Without stopping, and without lifting the pen off the paper, draw ten large, messy circles that overlap each other. Do not try to make them round. Just let your hand move across the paper.
answer: You should see a tangled scribble of overlapping ink lines, with no blank spots and no neat geometry anywhere.
```

Let us practice letting go of our need for neatness right now. When you made those circles just now, did you feel the urge to pause and fix a bad curve? Did you feel a little knot of tension in your stomach when a line strayed off the page or went lopsided? That tension is your old habit trying to take control. 

Every time you catch yourself tensing up, relax your grip. Let your hand move freely across the page. Real drawing — the kind of drawing that lets you look at an everyday object in your room and capture it on paper — is messy by nature. Real ink lines are uneven. They wiggle. They double back on themselves. 

Let us say that again so it settles deep into your bones: real drawing is messy, uneven, and full of wiggles. Perfection is for printers and machines. Human beings draw with wobbles, and those wobbles are what give a drawing its life and its charm. 

```figure kind="show" alt="A close-up view of a hand holding a pen making a series of quick, looping scribbles on a flat pad of paper, with the wrist completely elevated above the page." src="01-embracing-the-wobble-why-mistakes-are-just-lines-3.jpg"
```

Notice how your wrist is resting. If you press the heel of your hand hard against the paper, your movement becomes restricted to a tiny circle governed only by your fingers. Lift your wrist slightly. Let your hand float just above the paper, supported lightly by the side of your hand or your pinky finger. This gives your arm the freedom to move smoothly across the page, making marks that are bold and unrestrained. 

Try making a few random marks right now. Make a zig-zag. Make a sweeping arc. Make a dense patch of scribbles where the ink builds up into a dark shadow. Do not judge a single mark as it lands on the paper. Just watch the ink appear, accept it instantly as a permanent part of the page, and move straight on to the next one. 

```exercise
Draw a continuous wavy line from the top of your paper all the way to the bottom, letting your hand shake and wobble as much as it wants. Do not lift the pen once.
answer: A long, meandering, delightfully crooked line that zig-zags down the page without any straight sections.
```

By refusing to use an eraser, you are teaching your brain a very valuable lesson: you must move forward. An eraser tells your brain that mistakes are dangerous and must be destroyed. A ballpoint pen with no eraser tells your brain a much kinder story: whatever you just drew belongs here now, so let us figure out what to do next. 

If a line goes wrong, you do not erase it. You simply draw another line right next to it or right through it. Real artists do not erase; they layer. They build up rich, complex networks of messy, uneven lines until the object they are looking at emerges from the chaos. 

Let us review what we have discovered in these first fifteen minutes. You hold your pen loosely, far back from the tip, with relaxed fingers and a floating wrist. You make marks on purpose, and when those marks turn out wobbly or uneven, you do not judge them or stop. You accept them instantly as real lines, because mistakes are not errors at all — they are simply the building blocks of drawing. 

```recap
You have learned that a ballpoint pen is meant to be held loosely, far back from the tip, with a soft hand and a floating wrist. You have practiced making deliberate marks without stopping to judge them, and you now understand that messy, uneven, wobbly lines are not mistakes to be erased, but the very essence of real drawing.
```
